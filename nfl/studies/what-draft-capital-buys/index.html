Does draft capital still matter after a rookie season?

Verdict — For the middle, and it buys snaps

For one group, decisively. Among rookies who caught between 1.0 and 2.5 a game, the ones drafted in the first three rounds gained +0.98 catches a game in year two and the later picks -0.07 — from the same rookie production.

That a first-round receiver out-produces an undrafted one as a rookie is not news, and it is not a finding either: he was given the job. The question that decides a trade is whether where he was drafted still tells you anything once you have watched him play a season.

It buys playing time, not improvement. In that middle band the early picks gained 11.8 points of snap share and the late picks 1.2. Nothing in the efficiency numbers separates them. A team that spent a premium pick keeps giving him the ball; a team that spent a sixth-rounder moves on to the next one.

And it only matters in the middle. A rookie who barely played gains about six tenths of a catch either way — there is so much room above him that the round stops mattering. A rookie who already produced heavily gains little either way, because he already has the job. The draft slot decides the ambiguous cases, which is exactly where anyone actually has to make a call.

Running backs point the same way and are not counted here. The band that is supposed to hold rookie production equal does not do so for backs: inside it the early picks averaged 10.7 carries a game and the later picks 7.1, so the comparison is busier rookies against quieter ones and the draft round is along for the ride. Narrowing to six-to-twelve carries balances the groups and the gap holds, at +2.5 against −1.3 — but that band was chosen after trying several, so it is reported as directional rather than as a third confirmation.

The practical reading: after one season, a mid-round receiver who flashed is a worse bet than a first-rounder who flashed by exactly the same amount, and the reason has nothing to do with either of them.

How this was measured

Every receiver and tight end with at least six games in his rookie season and in the following one, 2016–2025, compared against HIMSELF. Players are grouped by what they produced as rookies FIRST and split by draft round second, so the two groups enter the comparison having done the same thing — the table shows that balance rather than asserting it. Rounds 1–3 count as early; round 4, round 7 and undrafted all count as late.
